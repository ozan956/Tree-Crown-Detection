\begin{abstract}
Global warming, loss of biodiversity, and air pollution are among the most significant problems facing Earth, and monitoring forests is central to addressing them. Automatic tree crown detection from satellite imagery is therefore an important task, approached by both traditional image-processing and deep-learning methods, usually in isolation. This study asks a specific question: once a strong ensemble of deep detectors has been applied, can a traditional method still contribute, and if so, what exactly does it add? We combine five deep detectors through weighted boxes fusion (WBF) with a traditional pipeline --- an illumination-invariant color feature and a Gabor filter bank feeding a watershed and local-maxima detector --- using a rule-based integration procedure. Evaluating on a benchmark of 222 satellite images containing 13{,}552 trees under one-to-one matching, we report precision, recall, and $F_1$ for every method. Our central finding is that the traditional operator recovers 448 true crowns (3.3 percentage points) lying \emph{beyond the detection ceiling of the full six-model deep-learning stack at its most permissive thresholds} --- trees that no detector proposes at any confidence. These recovered crowns are systematically small (a median crown area $2.44\times$ below detected crowns) and spectrally faint, which explains why appearance-trained detectors miss them and a texture and local-maxima operator recovers them. A lightweight appearance gate on the traditional-only detections removes the non-tree false positives they introduce, yielding a recall-maximizing operating point that, at an $F_1$ statistically tied with the ensemble, detects substantially more trees (recall $0.96$ versus $0.88$). On an independent benchmark from a different biome and sensor (US airborne forests, using the field-standard DeepForest detector), the same mechanism replicates: the recovered crowns are again the small ones ($1.91\times$ smaller). Traditional methods thus retain a specific, quantifiable value in the deep-learning era.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sect:intro}

Forests and terrestrial plants produce $28\%$ of Earth's oxygen, making trees vital for sustainability \cite{GFRA}. Uncontrolled destruction of trees is one of the main causes of recent global issues such as global warming, air pollution, desertification, and intensified natural disasters \cite{forest_climate_change, forest_carbon_emission}. Human activities, including agriculture, livestock grazing, and urban expansion, are the primary causes, leading to continuous forest decline over the past 30 years. Despite legal attempts, only 18\% of forests are currently under protection \cite{GFRA, brazilian_law, turkey_law_1, turkey_law_2}. Monitoring methods such as LiDAR scanning, drone-based analysis, and field sampling have limitations and are difficult to scale. Correctly inventorying trees requires knowing their location and canopy area; tree density reflects forest health and carbon storage capacity, and canopy width reflects photosynthesis potential. Detecting individual tree crowns is therefore crucial, and traditional and deep-learning methods applied to satellite imagery can provide an efficient solution.

Traditional and deep-learning methods for tree crown detection are usually used separately. Traditional methods, such as template matching and local-maxima techniques, have shown notable success \cite{Korpela20032006, huo2020individual}, but struggle with small crowns whose limited detail impedes detection \cite{Korpela20032006}, and Larsen~\emph{et al.}~\cite{larsen2011comparison} showed that traditional algorithms alone are insufficient across regions. Deep-learning object detectors are highly effective for tree detection; swin transformers, RCNN variants, YOLO, and DETR have all been analyzed for this task and shown complementary strengths \cite{durgut2025multi}, and combinations of swin transformers with U-Net-like architectures improve detection and segmentation \cite{xiao2023csswin, ren2024local}.

Despite the individual success of both families, studies combining their results remain scarce. Niccolai~\emph{et al.}~\cite{niccolai2010decision} used a rule-based algorithm with local neighborhood analysis, and hybrid methods integrating traditional segmentation with CNNs report improved accuracy and complementary strengths \cite{korznikov2021using, moussaid2021tree}. These works, however, assert complementarity qualitatively. To our knowledge, no prior study \emph{quantifies}, on a labeled benchmark, how many trees a traditional operator recovers beyond a strong deep-learning ensemble, nor characterizes \emph{which} trees those are.

This study leverages the strengths of both families within a rule-based integration and, crucially, measures and explains the complementarity between them. Five deep detectors are fused with weighted boxes fusion (WBF); a traditional pipeline extracts color and texture features and detects crowns through a joint probability map and watershed segmentation; and a rule-based procedure integrates the two.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A quantified, benchmark-backed measurement of the \emph{complementarity} between traditional and deep detectors for tree crowns: a traditional operator recovers 3.3\% of trees beyond the full six-model deep-ensemble ceiling, and we explain why --- the recovered crowns are systematically small and spectrally faint.
  \item A demonstration that the mechanism generalizes to an independent benchmark from a different biome and sensor (US airborne forests) using the field-standard DeepForest detector: the recovered crowns are again the small ones.
  \item A rule-based integration procedure with a lightweight appearance gate that removes the false positives the traditional step introduces, exposing a tunable recall/precision operating curve, and a rigorous evaluation protocol (one-to-one matching with precision, recall, $F_1$, and mean average precision, with bootstrap confidence intervals on the headline comparison).
\end{itemize}

\section{Traditional Methods for Tree Crown Detection}

Traditional methods are useful for object detection in satellite images, particularly when computational power and labeled data are limited, and they complement deep-learning detectors. Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow} shows the traditional workflow; we describe each step below.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{fig_workflow.pdf}
\caption{Workflow of the traditional tree crown detection pipeline: two per-pixel features (illumination-invariant color and a Gabor filter bank) form a joint probability map, which is thresholded and segmented by watershed; local maxima of the distance transform, merged at crown scale, give tree centers and approximate boundaries.}\label{fig:workflow}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

We compute a feature value for each pixel and use these values to select pixels carrying tree-crown characteristics. We extract two complementary features: an illumination-invariant color feature and a Gabor texture feature.

\subsubsection{Illumination-invariant Color}

To extract vegetation color robustly, we work in normalized-rgb (chromaticity) space, in which each channel is divided by the per-pixel intensity sum, making the representation invariant to overall brightness and shading --- essential given the strong sun and shadow variation in satellite imagery. On these chromaticity coordinates we compute an excess-green index, $2g - r - b$, as the color evidence $C(x,y)$ for a tree crown. Because it does not depend on absolute brightness, this feature is stable across illumination changes within a scene.

\subsubsection{Gabor Filter Bank}

The second feature captures local texture. Following the systematic filter-selection scheme of Jain and Farrokhnia~\cite{JAIN19911167}, each channel is filtered with a bank of Gabor filters spanning four orientations ($0^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ, 135^\circ$) and five radial center frequencies spaced by $\sqrt{2}$, so the bank uniformly covers the spatial-frequency domain. Each response is passed through a nonlinear transform and its energy pooled in a local window; the texture feature $G(x,y)$ is the average of the pooled responses.

\subsection{Joint Probability Map and Crown Detection}

Following the logic of \cite{ozcan_abdullah}, we form a probability map $J(x,y) = w_1 \, C(x,y) + w_2 \, G(x,y)$, in which each element estimates the probability that a pixel belongs to a tree crown. The color feature separates tree pixels substantially better than texture on high-resolution satellite imagery (pixel-level AUC $0.86$ versus $0.67$; Section~\ref{sec:results}), so we weight color more heavily ($w_1=0.9$, $w_2=0.1$); the Gabor bank still contributes a small complementary gain and is retained. We normalize $J$ to $[0,255]$, apply Otsu's thresholding and an opening operation to remove small vegetation patches, run watershed segmentation, and extract local maxima of the distance transform as candidate tree locations. Local maxima closer than a crown-scale distance are merged so that one crown yields one center.

\section{Deep Learning Methods for Tree Crown Detection}\label{sec:deep_learning_models}

We summarize the deep-learning detectors used and the WBF procedure that merges their outputs.

\subsection{Swin Transformers}
Liu~\emph{et al.}~\cite{liu2021swin} introduced swin transformers, whose shifted-window self-attention gives linear complexity with image size and is effective at detecting crowns of varying size.

\subsection{Faster RCNN}
RCNN combines CNNs with region-based approaches \cite{girshick2014rich}. Faster RCNN adds a region proposal network and RoI pooling \cite{ren2015faster}, lowering inference time and performing well on small objects \cite{nguyen2020evaluation,liu2021survey}.

\subsection{YOLO}
YOLO detects objects in a single pass \cite{redmon2016you}. We use YOLOv3, whose feature pyramid network aids small-object detection \cite{redmon2018yolov3, wang2022remote}.

\subsection{Deformable DETR}
DETR is a set-prediction transformer \cite{carion2020end}; Deformable DETR attends to a small set of key locations, enabling more sensitive detection of small objects \cite{zhu2020deformable}.

\subsection{Weighted Boxes Fusion}
WBF combines the outputs of multiple detectors \cite{solovyev2021weighted}. Low-scoring boxes are kept --- we take the maximum confidence when fusing, so they do not lower the final score but help capture unique trees --- and boxes detecting the same object are clustered by intersection over union and their coordinates recomputed as a confidence-weighted average,
\begin{eqnarray}
  x_{1,2} &=& \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{T} c(i) x_{1,2}(i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{T} c(i)}, \qquad
  y_{1,2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{T} c(i) y_{1,2}(i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{T} c(i)},
\end{eqnarray}
after which the confidence is scaled by how many models detected the object, $c(i) = c(i)\,\min(T,N)/N$.

\section{Rule-based Integration}\label{section:rule-based}

We integrate the two sources in four steps. Deep-learning boxes with confidence $\geq \tau_a = 0.8$ are treated as reliable, justified by the accuracy gains from multi-model fusion. For each traditional tree-center candidate we (i) accept it if it lies within a reliable box; (ii) otherwise accept it if it has at least $N_n$ reliable neighbors within distance $\tau_d$; (iii) otherwise examine the local joint-probability map and accept the candidate if a crown-sized contour supports it. This procedure validates deep detections and adds traditional-only detections where the detectors are silent.

\paragraph{Appearance gate.} Traditional-only candidates far from any reliable box can fire on non-tree texture (grass, bare soil, rooftops). We therefore add a lightweight appearance gate applied only to these far-from-box candidates: a crown-sized crop is described by eight cheap features --- mean brightness, saturation, excess-green, greenness, local texture (intensity standard deviation), and mean R/G/B --- and a logistic classifier decides tree versus non-tree. Candidates near a reliable box bypass the gate. The gate is trained on a labeled subset and evaluated on image-disjoint data (Section~\ref{sec:results}); its acceptance threshold provides a tunable trade-off between recovering faint trees and rejecting non-tree texture.

\section{Experiments and Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Dataset and Environment}
We use the VHRTrees dataset of 1471 satellite images \cite{10.3389/ffgc.2024.1495544}, covering Bursa (Marmara region; dry-summer temperate, oaks and beeches) and İzmir (Mediterranean; pines and olives), split into 1023 training, 226 validation, and 222 test images containing 60{,}356 / 13{,}227 / 13{,}552 trees. Deep detectors were trained with MMDetection \cite{chen2019mmdetection} for 100 epochs; WBF, the traditional pipeline, and the integration run on a laptop (NVIDIA RTX A2000, 16~GB RAM).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} We evaluate every method under \emph{one-to-one} (Hungarian) matching between predictions and ground-truth crowns, so false positives are penalized. We report precision, recall, and $F_1$ at the operating point used by the integration (detector score $\geq 0.8$), and COCO mean average precision (AP, AP$_{50}$) over all detections. The $0.8$ threshold is inherited from the deep-learning filtering stage; the WBF ensemble's $F_1$ is essentially flat ($0.921$--$0.925$) across thresholds $0.5$--$0.8$. We place $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals (2000 resamples over test images) on the headline comparison.

\subsection{Detection Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports all methods and Fig.~\ref{fig:pr_space} places them in precision--recall space. Faster R-CNN is the best single detector ($F_1$ $0.892$); the WBF ensemble improves over every single model ($F_1$ $0.921$, recall $0.878$ at precision $0.968$), confirming the value of multi-model fusion. All detectors saturate near AP$_{50}\approx 0.85$; the ensemble's advantage is recall at high precision.

\subsection{Recovery Beyond the Deep-Ensemble Ceiling}

The central question is whether the traditional step adds detections the deep ensemble fundamentally cannot produce, as opposed to detections a lower threshold would also yield. We take the union of all six detectors' boxes at their \emph{most permissive} score thresholds --- the theoretical ceiling of the deep-learning stack --- and measure ground-truth coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). This union reaches $94.2\%$ of trees; the WBF ensemble $92.5\%$; the best single detector at most $86.9\%$. The integrated method reaches $97.5\%$, recovering a net $448$ crowns ($3.3\%$) that \emph{no} detector proposes at \emph{any} confidence. The objection ``lower the detector threshold'' is therefore insufficient: every detector is already at its floor.

Grey bars are the five individual detectors at their most permissive score thresholds (all near $86\%$); the blue bar is the WBF ensemble ($92.5\%$); the green bar is the union of all six detectors at their floors, i.e.\ the theoretical deep-learning ceiling ($94.2\%$); and the orange bar is the integrated method ($97.5\%$). The integrated method reaches $3.3$ percentage points beyond the union ceiling --- trees no detector proposes at any confidence.}\label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the detectors miss these crowns.} Fig.~\ref{fig:beyond} compares the beyond-ceiling crowns to the detected population. Beyond-ceiling crowns have a median area of $788$\,px$^2$ versus $1924$\,px$^2$ for detected crowns ($2.44\times$ smaller; $82\%$ below the 25th percentile of detected-crown area) and are brighter, less saturated, and lower in excess-green --- small and spectrally faint --- yet retain higher local texture contrast. Appearance-trained detectors are least confident on small faint crowns and drop them below any usable threshold, whereas a texture and local-maxima operator, not relying on learned appearance priors, still fires. Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} shows these recoveries on example images: the recovered crowns (red) are consistently the small, faint ones the detectors (blue) missed.

\subsection{The Appearance Gate: Recall at Matched $F_1$}

Recovering faint crowns also introduces false positives: traditional-only detections fire on grass, bare soil, and rooftops, so the integrated method trades precision ($0.833$) for recall ($0.971$). Of its $2{,}647$ false positives, $85\%$ are traditional-only points far from any reliable box. The appearance gate (Section~\ref{section:rule-based}), trained and evaluated on image-disjoint halves of the test set, has a tunable threshold that trades precision against retention of the beyond-ceiling recoveries (Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}). At the operating point that preserves $94\%$ of the recoveries, the gate raises precision to $0.893$ at recall $0.957$, for $F_1$ $0.924$.

Under $2000$-resample image-level bootstrap, the integrated-plus-gate method and the WBF ensemble are statistically indistinguishable in $F_1$ (difference $95\%$ CI $[-0.019, +0.007]$). The recall difference, however, is large and unambiguous: the integrated method's recall exceeds the ensemble's by $+5.6$ to $+9.0$ points ($95\%$ CI, positive in $100\%$ of resamples). The contribution is therefore best stated precisely: \emph{at no cost in overall $F_1$, the integration detects substantially more trees than the ensemble} --- valuable when completeness of the inventory matters more than a few extra false positives.

\subsection{Generalization Across Scenes}
Using scene identifiers in the test set as an acquisition/region proxy, we test whether the findings hold on unseen scenes (Fig.~\ref{fig:scene}). The beyond-ceiling recovery is a \emph{targeted} benefit: its per-scene gain correlates $-0.98$ with detector strength, i.e.\ it helps most where the detectors are weakest ($+10$ to $+18$ pp on low-coverage scenes) and is neutral where they saturate. In a leave-one-scene-group-out test, the appearance gate improves $F_1$ on 7 of 9 held-out scene groups, so it is not merely fit to the evaluation data.

\subsection{Generalization Across Datasets: NEON}
To test whether the mechanism generalizes beyond VHRTrees, we evaluate on the NeonTreeEvaluation benchmark (109 annotated RGB tiles, $3{,}762$ trees, US NEON forest sites; airborne imagery, a different biome and sensor). As the deep detector we use DeepForest, the field-standard NEON-trained tree detector, so it is competent on its home data and there is no domain-shift confound. DeepForest reaches $78.4\%$ of trees; adding the traditional operator raises union coverage, and the recovered crowns are again the small ones --- median area $1.91\times$ smaller than DeepForest-reached crowns (Fig.~\ref{fig:neon}). The small-crown recovery mechanism thus replicates across continent, sensor, and detector. We are explicit about the boundary: the traditional pipeline's parameters were tuned on VHRTrees, and its standalone precision on NEON is low ($0.25$; it over-fires on dense conifer canopy). The deployable cross-dataset claim is therefore the \emph{mechanism} --- that the trees beyond the detector are the small ones --- rather than a turnkey coverage gain; applying the pipeline to a new biome requires recalibrating its crown-scale and threshold parameters and re-fitting the appearance gate.

\subsection{Comparison with a Density-map Counter}\label{sec:density}
Density-map regression is a common modern approach for dense small-object counting, so we compare against it. We train a CSRNet-style density counter on the VHRTrees training split and evaluate it identically (Table~\ref{tab:results}). It is a strong \emph{counter} (test count mean absolute error $4.63$ trees/image) but a weaker \emph{detector}: its best detection $F_1$ is $0.860$ (peak-decode thresholds tuned for best $F_1$), below both the ensemble and the integrated method. Density regression blurs instances into a continuous surface to count robustly; recovering discrete, well-localized centers from that surface is lossy, especially where crowns merge. This supports a detection-centric design for individual-tree localization, while density methods remain competitive when the goal is counting. The density counter is a single training run and its $F_1$ should be read as a competent lower bound rather than an exhaustively tuned ceiling.

\subsection{Discussion}
A traditional operator contributes detections a strong deep ensemble cannot, and these are specifically the small, faint crowns; a simple appearance gate converts the resulting recall gain into an operating point that matches the ensemble's $F_1$ while detecting substantially more trees. The complementarity is a targeted safety-net, most valuable where deep detection is weakest --- precisely where a practitioner needs it. Beyond detection, the traditional segmentation supplies canopy information and can act as an automatic, partial labeler for unlabeled imagery, on which deep models can be trained and the rule-based step reapplied to refine results.

\subsection{Limitations}
Three limitations bound our claims. First, while the mechanism replicates on NEON, the specific magnitude ($3.3$ pp) and the appearance-gate coefficients are dataset-dependent, and the traditional pipeline requires per-biome recalibration (its untuned NEON precision is $0.25$). Second, ``beyond-ceiling'' is defined relative to these six detectors (or DeepForest on NEON); a detector trained specifically for tiny crowns could shrink the gap, so the finding characterizes the complementarity of current strong detectors rather than a permanent limit. The scene-holdout generalization uses image identifiers as a proxy for acquisition and region; these are not confirmed climate labels, so that experiment measures cross-scene rather than cross-climate transfer within the dataset. Third, the appearance gate is supervised; for unlabeled imagery it must be trained once on a labeled subset, which the image-disjoint evaluation is designed to estimate.

\section{Conclusion}
We combined traditional and deep-learning methods for tree crown detection through a rule-based integration and measured the complementarity between them rather than reporting a single aggregate score. A traditional operator recovers $3.3\%$ of trees beyond the ceiling of a six-model deep ensemble; these are systematically small, faint crowns; an appearance gate makes the integrated detector match the ensemble's $F_1$ while detecting substantially more trees; and the recovery mechanism replicates on an independent US airborne benchmark with the field-standard detector. Traditional methods thus retain a specific, quantifiable value in the deep-learning era: recovering the small crowns that appearance-trained detectors systematically miss.
